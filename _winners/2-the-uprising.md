Sweat beaded on the temple of Reverend Bradshaw as he hurried to latch the lock on the basement door. The parsonage groaned with the sounds of stretching wood, and the Reverend slumped against the unfinished walls. The Broughton winter burrowed beneath his skin, even under all this soil.

Broughton was a quiet settlement north of Lake George. Before he was born, James Bradshaw’s parents were born in Lancashire and among the first to arrive in the New World. Though they landed in Massachusetts, it was he, with his congregation of fifty, that ventured up to Vermont. Bordering the discovered land of the Americas, Broughton liked to think of itself as a soldier in the holy quest of purifying the uncharted west. They fought the savage Indians, toughed unknowable lands, and had since grown to almost two hundred.

Out of nowhere, the earth seemed to breathe. Bradshaw turned the knife in his hand, and muttered a psalm. Dust fell down from the ceiling.

Broughton’s Puritan values were the “soul of our community,” Bradshaw liked to say. Discipline, honor, and tradition were law in the small town. Children, he often thought, are most prone to straying from these ideals. The malleable minds of their youngest were to be protected, lest they succumb to the Devil’s whims. When children were found misbehaving, they would be punished. The lash of a whip, hours kneeling on sharp rocks, a punch, a kick—the children knew to never step out of line. As rumors of witches swept New England, Bradshaw delighted in the knowledge that his congregation knew better, trusted too much in the Lord, to fall to such wicked fads.

Footsteps fell, maybe five sets, maybe a thousand. It was outside the parsonage now. The Reverend chose to blow out the small candle in fear that the light would leak through the rickety ceiling above. Still, he shuddered as the darkness left him alone with the damp soil. Damp soil and a million tiny footsteps.

The only time Broughton allowed for music outside of church was in weddings and funerals. Weddings were the only event that Bradshaw would be seen smiling at. While some towns had looser regulations around marriage, young people were encouraged to wed since Bradshaw had the desire to grow, grow, grow their town. While Bradshaw never openly condemned the unmarried, he preached encouraging the young folk, especially girls, to do their service in bringing into the world more children of God. Funerals, on the other hand, were expectedly a more somber affair. Broughton living came with many struggles. The fields were hard work, the changing seasons unforgiving. There were many hardships in the congregation’s efforts to tame such a wild land.

The door blew open and Bradshaw’s muttering turned into a trembling scream—

“Fear thou not, for I am with thee: be not afraid—!”

A hand from the mass reached not to cover his mouth but to grab inside it. It was copper-tasting and slippery and inhumanly firm in its grasp. Bradshaw’s eyes snapped open.

At times, it became the duty of the people of Broughton to ensure the cleanliness and holiness of the town. It was Bradshaw who oversaw the trial, and then, often, executions of the unsavable sinner. Murderers, thieves, drunks, jezebels… Bradshaw, of course, did not handle the executions himself.

The mass screeched. In the flurry of faces, Bradshaw thought he saw the face of young Goody Anderson, though he was sure she had passed last winter. It had grabbed his arm, slithered behind him. A mess of bird-boned limbs poking into his ribs and neck and back. There too was the face of Jonathan, Betty, Harlow, and Thomas. The youngest Smith, the oldest Miller. The children were smiling, they were singing. He couldn’t quite tell where one ended and the other began.

Smoke filled the room, though there didn’t seem to be a fire. If anything, the room was cold, cold, cold, and getting colder.

The last thing that Bradshaw saw was the face of a girl he had condemned just a week before. A girl of thirteen years had tempted one of the honest men of his congregation, a splotch on Bradshaw’s near perfect record. The noose still hung limp around her neck. She pulled back an axe and it lodged into his chest with a sickening thud.

A scream pierced through the purple-black evening, startling a family of sparrows out of their tree. The sky expanded.

Broughton was again wild and free.
