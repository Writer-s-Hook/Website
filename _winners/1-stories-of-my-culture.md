Dumplings.

From the safety of the window, I watch Mom pinch each one to doughy perfection. She leans over to my aunties, muttering critiques about my little cousins as she watches them clumsily push filling into each one. When no one is looking, she takes each one from the finished pile and remolds it. No one could ever fit her standards.

I stand outside the little house that used to be home. The downpour—heavy and cold—sloshes over the thin roof ledge, plastering my dyed blonde hair to my head.

It’s not that I’m not excited to see everyone again. It’s just that—

My mother looks up from over the kitchen sink, her dark eyes wordlessly finding mine. She dusts the flour from her fingers, and with no small effort, slides open the rusting screen door separating us.

“Come. Come, Emily,” she murmurs, tugging on my sleeve. “Why you stand in the cold? Eesh, you so skinny. There food inside for you. Come. Come.”

Some things never change.

I smile despite myself as I wipe my feet off on the doormat and slip off my rain boots. She ushers me into the kitchen, where several of my aunties greet me with a traditional 恭喜发 财. I wash my hands, the water further numbing my fingers. Dad never did get around to fixing that “damn water heater.”

I take my place beside Mom. And I start counting. One second. Two seconds. Three. Then the questions come.

“Why you dye your hair? And cut it so short? It was pretty as it was.” Auntie Jin’s eyes narrow in dissatisfaction, her words coming out more clipped than she intends.

My other auntie, Julie, chimes in with her own critique. “Why you don’t come visit us more often? Haven’t seen you since you started your medical program. Avoiding us?”

I inhale deeply. They don’t know. Of course they don’t know. “I…um…I quit. It wasn’t—I mean, it never made me happy. It’s not what I wanted to do with my life.”

Work stalls. For the first time, my aunts and my mother stand completely frozen in place, just staring. The only sound is my baby cousins slapping the dough and pushing the broken pieces back together.

“What did you say?” It’s my mother’s voice.

“I quit,” I say, my voice shaking. “It-it didn’t make me happy. But I started this writing program, and I—”

“A writing program?” Auntie Julie shakes her head. “A writing program doesn’t pay the bills, girl. The goal was always doctor. We worked for you to be a doctor.”

I know.

I remember late nights. Math notes spilling over the dining room table, the numbers illuminated by flickering flashlights from Dollar Tree. The slipper after report cards. Her framed pictures of mathematicians, engineers, and doctors, all glaring from the mantel all the while.

I don’t say this. I know better.

“I remember,” I mutter.

She retorts, eyes flashing, “You know how long we work for you Ài mǐ lì? Your mom come from China with clothes on her back, and you in her belly, just for you to do this?! No! You finish program, and then you come home. And you marry; I will find you good husband.”

Oh right. The other bombshell.

“I… don’t like boys.”

“Of course not. You like men. But right now, you acting like little girl, so you find no man, only boy.”

“No, I like girls. I’m lesbian.”

Mom stares at me, turning to the other aunties. “That a disease? Lebian?”

“You know what? Don’t worry,” Auntie Jin says. “Cure disease with new doctor money.” The other aunties nod their agreement.

“Mom, I will never have a husband. I just…I don’t see men that way.” I knead the dough with more force, doing everything I can to avoid my mother’s piercing gaze.

Auntie Julie scoffs. “You let this one run too wild, Joy. Back in China, this never would be allowed.”

“Be quiet, Julie,” Mom snaps.

A pin could’ve dropped. Of course, nobody would’ve heard it over their screaming.

“‘Be quiet?’”

“Your daughter gone crazy!”

“Coming here, looking like a white girl.”

“Saying she won’t take a husband because she likes girls!”

“Dropping scholarship to chase some silly dream!”

“She my daughter! Not yours! This why your children don’t want to visit you anymore!” My mom turns to me. She’s stiff, composed, but her eyes are soft. “I’m happy you visit me. Tell me about this program. And tell me more about this special girl.”
